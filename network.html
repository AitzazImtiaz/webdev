Who owns the wires?

In Korea, almost three network companies hold entire fixed-broadband market. KT which is the former state monopooly, almost held around 41% of subscribers in 2023, roughly amounting to 9.8 million households. SK broadband, owned by SK, which was consequentially bought from Hanaro Telecom in 2008, held about 29%. LG U+ held around 21%, making the other remaining fraction owned by cable system operators.

An inference that I pick from here is consequential fact that Korean internet roster relies on three providers, explaining a lot of information elsewhere on this site. The correlation That I am trying to make essentially is, a routing error from one company in October 2021 could cut off service to 16.5 million customers at once, blocking them from using card payments, assessing clinical record systems, and attending online classes in Covid era. At the time, for internet access, 14.3 million people depended on KT!

Subsequentially, this is why peering disputes matter which I will discuss on Culture page. If three companies own cables, negotiations about who pays for traffic, especially when considering their shares, does not become a negotiation between equals.

Where traffic meets

In retro vintage times, era dated before 1995 when internet was developing as a utility and ARPANET was just decomissioned to broad the technology, meant Korean traffic between two networks was routed across Pacific, being back-and-forth slow and expensive, worsening as users grew every year. The government realized this and so Korea Computerization Agency built the country's first domesticated exchange system in 1995, which unfortunately they could not keep operating, so it was handled over to private hands; LG U+ getting DIX, and KT getting KTIX.

By the time ISPs were forming in Korea, this led to a awkward situation where carrier-run exchanges had to react. Sixteen of them responded in forming KINX in June 1999, the Korea Internet Neutral eXchange. It came to be the persistent, still modern day, only neutral exchange in Korea and half of its members are now international.

Modern day Korea runs atleast five non-trivial exchange points. Network reaches outside world through nine submarine cables landing at four coastal stations with additional capacity enhanced in 2004.

But why neutrality matters?

This is an important inference to make here, carrier exchange and their operations exposes a market which is influenced with views of leverage and concern with traffic belonging to rivals. A neutral exchange should be born, which jointly governs and being settlement-free, focuses on improvement of the internet overall, the reason being sixteen competing providers were willing to build upon it together.

KINX is only neutral exchange in the country and has been since 1999. It has survived since origin of internet and does not compete.

Fast, as said, no longer exceptional

Korea earned its fame earlier on per se. By 2006 94% of hoursehold residents had broadband connection, which was highest rate in OECD, roughly being fifty five times that of Turkey. Five years later, Akamai measured Korea as fastest country on earthwhich averaged 17.5 Mbps compared against United States 5.8 Mbps of that time.

Absolute numbers in general climbed in Korea, as noted around end of 2025, when median fixed download speed reached 233 Mbps, which is a 47.9% hike in a single year! However, Korea did fall from these relative positions when fibre cables got widespread, and Korea stopped defining these tables when other countries with higher budgets pushed forth 5G.

This is a non-trivial take, and one around this site is build in my opinion. Physical network was built earlier on, with remaining story of platform, policy and culture including the emphasis on the social boom.
